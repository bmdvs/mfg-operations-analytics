Executive Summary

A discrete event simulation of the shop floor was built to test a range of scenarios aimed at raising on-time delivery. The model replays every job released from January 2024 through the shop's own routings, shift calendars, crewing, recorded downtime, and dispatch rules, and draws setup, run, move, and material times from the shop's recorded performance. This lets us measure the impact that each scenario has on on-time delivery and related measures: brake utilization and queue time, floor WIP, late jobs, and days late.

The model's accuracy was checked with a baseline run of every job with no changes, replicating the shop as it actually operated (the "Status Quo" scenario). It reproduced 100% of measures within tolerance, including 2025 on-time delivery of 80.2% against 80.8% measured and a year-end peak of 464 jobs on the floor against 466 measured. Section 4 gives the full validation.

We tested scenarios in two categories: (i) release and dispatch rules governing when work is released and the order in which it is run, and (ii) capacity changes that add machine hours. As shown below, the changes that add brake hours raised on-time delivery the most, while changes to release and dispatch had a neutral or negative impact.

Scenario Results: Change in On-time Delivery vs. Status Quo, 2025

We compared the results of the scenarios individually and in specific combinations alongside their cost of implementation, NPV and payback periods. We found that the "Operating Package" (brake setup program + additional Saturday shifts on both brakes) increased 2025 on-time delivery by 7.7 points to 87.9% without significant capital investment. Adding a robotic bending cell on top of the Operating Package generated the highest return of all capital-intensive options tested, increasing on-time delivery another 2.2 points to 90.1%. Sections 2 and 3 detail these results and ROI.

1. Simulations Tested

We considered two broad categories of simulations intended to raise on-time delivery:

Release and dispatch scenarios change the rules that govern how work enters and moves through the floor. These changes cost nothing to implement. If late jobs come from too much work on the floor, or from the wrong job going next at the brakes, these should move on-time delivery.

Capacity scenarios add machine hours through operating changes or new equipment. These changes cost labor and/or capital. The capacity report finds that the brakes are the shop's primary constraint: they operate at the highest utilization with the least buffer, and hold 44% of all queue days. Given this, we expect scenarios that add brake hours to move on-time delivery.

Each scenario changes only the inputs listed for it in the tables below; everything else stays as the shop operates today (the Status Quo). Each scenario and the Status Quo were run 30 times, each run with its own draws of setup, run, move and material times. Section 2 presents the results. 2. Results

Each scenario was evaluated based on its impact on on-time delivery rates in 2025 and Q2-Q4. As detailed in the flow report, this Q2-Q4 period excludes the significant capacity constraints and backlog build in Q1, and so presents a view of the scenario's performance in more normalized operating periods.

Each result in this section is a scenario's average over its 30 runs less the Status Quo's average over its 30 runs, with a 95% confidence interval: the range the true effect likely falls in. An effect is denoted positive or negative when the whole interval lies on one side of zero, and shows as no effect when the interval crosses zero.

Among the release and dispatch rules, only earliest due date improved on-time delivery. The other release and dispatch scenarios had a neutral to negative impact. The three WIP caps, the due-date ratio and shortest processing time at the brakes make on-time delivery worse, with the cap at 180 jobs reducing it by 9.4 points, because the caps move the wait from the brakes to the release gate (3.5 days held) without adding machine hours. See Appendix Table 3 for additional detail.

Release and Dispatch Scenarios: Change in On-Time Delivery vs. Status Quo

Scenarios that added brake capacity improved on-time delivery. The lower cost options, the brake setup program detailed in the capacity report and additional Saturday shifts on both brakes, added 4.5 points each to on-time delivery. The more capital intensive options, the robotic bending cell and replacing brake B3, also yielded improvements (9.4 and 4.7 points); they're analyzed in more detail in Section 3. The scenarios impacting capacity at the robotic weld and laser cutting centers yielded less improvement in on-time delivery, further validating the brakes as the shop's primary constraint.

Capacity Scenarios: Change in On-Time Delivery vs. Status Quo

Different combinations of scenarios were modelled, and one in particular yielded the best results. The "Operating Package" combines the brake setup program and additional Saturday shifts on both brakes, the two low cost capacity scenarios. This combination added 7.7 points to on-time delivery. On top of the Operating Package, the more capital-intensive scenarios were considered, with the robotic bending cell yielding the most significant further improvement to on-time delivery.

3. Return on the Changes That Work

Two decisions follow from the results, and they are separate. The operating package addresses the year-end build: it costs $60,000 once and $15,400 a year, and returns that in 0.4 years if half the 566 brake hours it frees are sold, for an NPV of $0.69M. On top of it the robotic cell returns $1.52M with a payback of 2.0 years and breaks even when 24% of its freed hours are sold; the tool changer does not reach zero at half sold (-$0.11M) and needs 57%. The weld cell second shift is the one lever that moves an ordinary quarter, at about $83,000 a year with no return in money. Nothing that costs money pays on cost savings alone.

What the changes cost and return

The return on each change is the brake hours it frees, if those hours are sold, plus overtime avoided, less what it costs to run. The operating package frees 566 brake hours a year, worth $170,000 at half sold, and adds $5,400 of Saturday overtime; the robotic cell frees a further 1,966 hours ($590,000) and costs $67,000 a year to run, $42,000 of it its operator; the tool changer frees 491 ($147,000). Overtime avoided is under $4,000 a year for every change. The simulation replays the 2025 orders, so it ships within 10 jobs of current practice under every option; the throughput value is an assumption about sales, not a simulated result, and only brake hours carry it. Operator hours freed without a change in hours paid are not counted as money. Planned Saturdays add hours rather than free them; their return is in on-time delivery, not in this table. Payback is the one-time cost over the net a year, and NPV is the net a year over 7 years at 10% less the one-time cost, before tax; rates and prices are the shop's. 4. Model Accuracy & Validation

How the model works

The model takes each job as the shop released it and moves it through its routing, machine by machine, under the shop's shift calendars and dispatch rules. What the record fixes, the model keeps: release dates, routings, standards, due dates and rush flags are those of the actual jobs, and machine downtime is the recorded downtime. What varies from job to job, how long each setup and run takes and how long a job waits for material, a move or outside processing, is drawn from the shop's 2025 distributions. The behaviours the record does not state as rules are estimated from it: how fully the brakes are crewed when work is waiting, and when and how the shop works a Saturday or an extended brake shift. Each scenario runs 30 times with different draws, and its effect is the difference from current practice, run for run, with a 95% interval.

How well it reproduces 2025

The model reproduces 2025 within tolerance on ten of the ten measures set before the model was run. Utilization is within 2.3 points of measured at every work center, and queue time per operation is within 25% at every work center but the punch (0.64 days against 1.06), which takes 815 first operations a year. Lead-time percentiles from the 10th to the 95th are within 10% of measured except the 10th for 2025 (4.8 days against 4.2). On-time delivery is 80.2% against 80.8% for 2025 and 90.3% against 91.3% in Q2-Q4, and the brakes hold 53% of queue time in the model against 53% in the record, with the wait for the powder color day left out of both, the capacity report's basis. The year-end build

The model builds and clears the year-end backlog as the shop did. Floor WIP peaks at 464 jobs against 466, both in the week of December 23, falls below 300 in the week of March 10 and below 250 in the week of March 24 in both, and below 220 in the week of April 7 against March 31. The brakes worked 5,345 hours from December through March against 5,339, 39 of them on Saturdays against 42, and jobs shipped in the first quarter were 52.2% on time against 52.4%. The model enters the autumn heavier than the shop, with 191 to 245 jobs on the floor in October against 175 to 191, and ships 2024 Q4 at 84.3% on time against 87.9%; it has converged by the week of November 18.

Three effects in this report are not exact, and the direction of each is known. The setup program and the tool changer are understated by about 6%, because 5.7% of brake operations carry their setup inside the run and leave nothing for either to shorten. Planned Saturdays with both brakes crewed are likely overstated: both brakes are worked at the weekday yield of 0.965, the record has three Saturdays with both brakes crewed, and its one-brake Saturdays yield 0.854. Effects in Q2-Q4 are slightly generous throughout, because the model ships those quarters at 90.3% against 91.3% and so has about 41 more late jobs there to remove.
