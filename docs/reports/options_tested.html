1. The Shop Model

On-time delivery is 77% against a 95% target because jobs wait at the brakes and because jobs are released late (Flow and On-time Delivery; Capacity, Constraints and Setups). This report tests what the shop could do about the first cause, on a model of the shop: which levers move on-time delivery, by how much, and at what cost. The model replays every job released from January 2024 to December 2025 with its routing, standards, due date, rush flag, shift calendar, recorded downtime and the powder color schedule, drawing setup, run, material, move, hold, outside-processing and ship times from their 2025 distributions. Each scenario runs 30 times; its effect is the paired difference from current practice, reported with a 95% interval, in working days with scheduled Saturdays counted.

The model reproduces 2025 within tolerance on 9 of 10 measures. Its one miss is the first-quarter backlog, which it clears faster than the shop did (59.8% on time in Q1 against 40.8% measured; every other quarter within 2.6 points), so effects on the first-quarter event are lower bounds. Its brakes run 2 points hotter than measured (93% against 91%), so the effects of capacity levers in ordinary quarters are slightly overstated. See Appendix Tables 1 and 2 for additional detail.

2. Holding Work Back and Reordering It Does Not Help

Capping release makes delivery worse at every level tested: a WIP cap at 240, 210 and 180 jobs lowers on-time delivery by 2.6, 6.1 and 20.9 points in Q2-Q4 (4.7, 8.4 and 22.0 for the year) and lengthens the 90th-percentile lead time by 2.0 to 5.7 days. The floor already carries less work than the quoted lead times allow in an ordinary quarter (the flow report), so a cap does not relieve the brakes; it moves the wait to the release gate, where jobs sat 1.3 to 4.2 days. Pacing release to the brake backlog is 1.8 points worse in Q2-Q4 for the same reason. See Appendix Tables 3 and 4 for additional detail.

Reordering the work does not help either. Earliest due date is indistinguishable from the current list for the year (+1.0 points, interval 0.0 to 2.0) and 0.5 points worse in Q2-Q4; critical ratio is 3.7 to 7.0 points worse; shortest processing time at the brakes shortens the median and is 2.0 to 2.2 points worse on time, shipping 21 fewer jobs. Running light work ahead of heavy on B1 and B2 (+0.0, interval -1.0 to +1.0) and a third weekly color day for black (+0.2, -0.9 to +1.2) move nothing beyond their intervals. The shop's dispatch list, with rush and hot-list precedence, is as good as any rule tested; release should not be capped and the list stays. See Appendix Tables 5 to 8 for additional detail.

3. Adding Hours at the Brakes Does

Every lever that adds or recovers brake hours raises on-time delivery, and three of them together, for $60,000, take the year from 82.0% to 88.0%. The setup program of the capacity report (the top 12 part-operations at standard, repeat setups to a familiar operator, a handover standard: 12.9 brake hours a week) is the largest single lever: +4.4 points (3.4 to 5.3) for the year, a 90th-percentile lead time 2.0 days shorter, 18 fewer jobs in WIP, and Saturday shifts down from 17 to 12. In Q2-Q4 it shortens the 90th percentile by 1.0 day but leaves on-time delivery where it was (+0.2, interval -0.1 to +0.5), because the late jobs of an ordinary quarter were released late rather than held at the brakes. A planned Saturday brake shift every week from November through February adds +1.5 points (0.3 to 2.8) for the year, a lower bound given the model's faster clearing of the backlog, for 4.0 more Saturday shifts than the queue-triggered practice produced. A second shift on the robotic weld cell adds +1.1 points (0.8 to 1.4) in Q2-Q4, the one lever that moves an ordinary quarter, and +0.8 (-0.3 to +2.0) for the year. See Appendix Table 9 for additional detail.

The three levers are close to additive, which is why they make a package: singly they sum to +6.7 points for the year, together they deliver +6.0 (5.0 to 6.9), reaching 88.0% on time with a 90th-percentile lead time of 19.8 days against 22.9. In Q2-Q4 the package reaches 91.8% (+1.2, 0.9 to 1.5) and 15.3 days, and all of that gain is the weld cell's second shift; planned Saturdays add nothing in those quarters because the peak they cover falls in Q4 and Q1. The package ships the same number of jobs for the year as current practice and 33 to 40 fewer in Q2-Q4, because the first-quarter backlog ships earlier under it. The package is three operating changes and $60,000: the setup program, a crewed second shift on the robotic weld cell, and a planned Saturday brake shift every week from November through February. It goes in first.

4. The Capital Options Against the Package

Three capital options were run on the same model: a press brake with automatic tool changing in place of B3 ($735,000), a second automated laser tower on L2 ($460,000) and a robotic bending cell as a sixth brake ($1,030,000). For the year the robotic cell raises on-time delivery by 8.1 points (7.3 to 9.0), the tool changer by 3.9 (2.9 to 5.0) and the tower by 1.1 (0.0 to 2.1), against 6.0 for the $60,000 package; in Q2-Q4 every capital option adds under a point (0.4, 0.0 and 0.4) against 1.2 for the package. The package delivers more than the tool changer and 73% of the cell's effect for 6% of the cell's price. See Appendix Tables 10 to 15 for additional detail.

Effect on On-time Delivery Against One-time Cost, 2025

Each machine does one thing. The tool changer removes tool-change time on one brake: 10.9 setup hours a week, about what the whole operating package recovers (11.1); covering all five brakes at the same price per machine would match the cell. The robotic cell adds a sixth brake for the light, repeat work in lots of 25 or more, 30% of brake standard hours; it works 46 hours a week, takes 39 a week off the manual brakes, removes 11.9 of 17 Saturday shifts and shortens the 90th-percentile lead time by 5.0 days, while adding 344 setup hours a year to the manual brakes, which keep the short lots. The laser tower clears the laser queue and changes nothing downstream: in the three weeks of the Q4 2024 build the jobs waiting at the lasers fall from 64 to 34 a day and the jobs released in those weeks ship 73.1% on time either way, because they then wait at the brakes; the package alone lifts those same jobs to 82.4%. On overtime and labor savings alone, no option pays back within seven years and the package is net negative ($70,000 a year, the second shift's labor). The case for any of them is the brake hours they free and whether those hours are sold. If half the freed brake hours are sold at a 40% margin, the robotic cell pays back in 1.6 years, the tool changer in 3.9 and the package in 0.6; the tower frees no brake hours and never pays back. The break-even is low for the cell and the package and high for the tool changer: NPV reaches zero at 15% of the cell's freed hours sold (308 a year), 24% of the package's (135) and 40% of the tool changer's (231). On top of the package, the cell adds 3.5 points for the year and the tool changer 1.8, with break-evens of 16% (324 hours a year) and 46% (241). Payback is the one-time cost over the net annual benefit; NPV discounts seven years at 10%, before tax, no residual value; rates and prices are the shop's. See Appendix Tables 17 to 23 for additional detail.

NPV Against the Share of Freed Brake Hours Sold

Order of decisions: the package first. The robotic bending cell when the shop can show that 324 of the brake hours it frees will be sold each year (308 without the package); it is the capital option with the largest effect and the lowest break-even. The tool changer on B3 only if 46% of its freed hours will be sold. The laser tower is not bought.

5. What the Floor Cannot Fix

With the package in place the year is at 88.0% and Q2-Q4 at 91.8%, 3 points short of the 95% target in an ordinary quarter. The remaining late jobs were released late, and no capacity lever reaches them: on the same floor, quoting from the load-based table of Quoting and Early Warning from Load (routing class and brake backlog at release, never below the fixed quote) lifts the year to 90.8% and Q2-Q4 to 92.7%, with 78% and 72% of non-rush promised lead times longer than the fixed quote, and does so without changing anything on the floor. Capacity closes the first-quarter gap; the quote closes most of what is left. See Appendix Table 24 for additional detail.

From Measured to Target: What Each Step Closes, 2025 and Q2-Q4

Three operating changes for $60,000 lift on-time delivery six points for the year; the quote table lifts it three more; the robotic cell is the one capital purchase worth making once its freed hours can be sold. Holding work back, reordering it, and clearing the laser queue do not move delivery, because the constraint is brake hours.
