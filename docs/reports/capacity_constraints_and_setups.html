1. Capacity Across the Shop

The shop's floor consists of 25 machines across ten work centers. The press brakes are the busiest, with 16,268 machine hours in 2025 (36% of total). Machine hours break into operations per day and hours per operation, shown in routing order below.

Daily Floor Activity by Work Center, 2025

The press brakes consistently run at the highest utilization of any work center (average of 83% 2023-2025), followed by the robotic weld cell (71%). Across all work centers, average utilization increased from 62% in 2023 to 75% in 2025 as released work grew 20% (4,315 to 5,157 jobs a year; brake standard hours up 21%) across the same set of machines and shifts.

Average monthly utilization by work center, 2023 to 2025

Capacity Constraints

In 2025, the press brakes ran at 91% average utilization and the robotic weld cell at 83%. Against their 97% uptime, the average production capacity buffer was only 6 points for the press brakes and 14 points for the robotic weld cell, the smallest of all work centers. See Appendix Table 1 for additional detail.

Utilization and Machine Uptime by Work Center

With so little buffer, an ordinary heavy week can take the brakes and the weld cell to maximum utilization: they ran at or above 95% utilization in 46% and 38% of weeks in 2025, far more often than any other work center. Average queue time rises sharply when a work center is operating near maximum utilization. This is explored in more detail in Section 2. See Appendix Table 2 for additional detail.

Share of weeks at or above 95% utilization, 2025
Queue Time in Weeks at or Above 95% Utilization vs. Other Weeks, 2023 to 2025

Throughout 2025, jobs spent 36,318 days waiting in queue at a work center (excluding first operations at the lasers and the punch). The press brake station accounted for 56% of those days, which is a function of its consistently high utilization, as well as its volume of operations. There were 5,692 press brake operations in 2025 (20% of total). The robotic weld cell, which also ran at high utilization, accounted for less than 5% of queue days, because only 423 operations (1.5% of total) passed through it.

Share of queue days by work center, 2025

The press brakes are therefore the shop's primary constraint on three counts: the highest utilization with the least buffer, the most weeks spent at maximum utilization, and more than half of all queue days. The robotic weld cell shares the first two and constrains the enclosure work that crosses it. These stations are examined in more detail throughout this report.

2. Load and Queue Time

At each station, average queue time was sharply higher when it was operating near maximum utilization compared to the other weeks of the year. The gap is smallest at the press brakes (2.4 days against 1.7, 1.4x) not because their wait is least sensitive to load but because their other weeks are also heavily loaded, so the comparison is narrower than other work centers. This section lays out the sensitivity of queue time against load at the press brakes and robotic weld stations.

Section 1 showed that queue time rises when a work center is full and that the press brakes and the robotic weld cell are the two that are full most often and carry the waiting that costs the shop days. This section shows how queue time builds at each as load rises, and what that makes the lever. The curve is the queue time expected at each level of utilization for the number of machines at the work center, fitted on rolling 13-week windows from 2023 to 2025 and drawn only over the range of utilization the work center has run at in those windows; for the brakes the fit explains 45% of the variation in the windowed queue with a rank correlation of 0.88, while a single week's queue carries the backlog of the weeks before it, which is why the fit is made on windows rather than weeks.

The press brakes

On the fitted curve the brake queue time is 0.67 days per operation at 75% utilization, 1.51 at 85%, 2.61 at 90% and 3.44 at 92%; the measured queue time is 3.54 at 91% for the year and 1.81 at 91% in Q2-Q4. Week by week, the brake queue time steps from about 0.5 days below 85% utilization to 2.7 above it and does not settle back. The brake queue time's 90th percentile was 17.1 and 9.1 days in Q1 and Q2 2025 and 2.8 and 3.0 in Q3 and Q4; under 5 days in every quarter is the mark the levers in this report are measured against. See Appendix Tables 3, 4 and 5 for additional detail.

Queue Time Against Utilization: Press brake (5 machines), 2023 to 2025

The brakes run at 91% and carry 56% of all queue time (Flow and On-time Delivery). B1 and B2 run at 92.2% and 91.9% for the year and 93.7% and 93.7% in Q2-Q4, with the shortest queue times of the five (mean 2.7 and 2.8 days): precision work goes to them first and the hot list sends expedited work to them. B3 to B5 carry the tail: mean 3.7 to 4.1 days and 90th percentile 11.6 to 12.7. Over all hours worked, B5 carries the highest load of the five for the year; in Q2-Q4 B1 and B2 do. On crewed weekday shifts only, B1 and B2 run at 96.6% and 96.3% for the year. See Appendix Tables 6 and 7 for additional detail.

Utilization and Queue Time by Brake, 2025

The brake queue carries over from week to week because arrivals are uneven and run times vary: daily arrivals have a coefficient of variation of 0.50, Mondays bring 1.77 times the daily mean and the top customer's month-end 1.73 times, and run time per operation varies far more than setup time (coefficient of variation 2.14 against 0.89). Second-shift absence is 7% against 3% on first shift. See Appendix Tables 8 and 9 for additional detail.

The robotic weld cell

The robotic weld cell runs at 83% on one shift and has the longest queue time in the shop in Q2-Q4, 3.9 days per operation against 1.8 at the brakes. As a single machine its wait climbs faster with load than the brakes': 5.4 days on the curve at 90% against 2.6 at the five brakes. It runs at or above 95% in 20 weeks of 52 and below 80% in 18, alternating between idle and saturated weeks rather than running steadily hot, and in 2025 it ran above its curve (3.64 days measured against 2.84).

Queue Time Against Utilization: Robotic weld (1 machine), 2023 to 2025

Enclosures are 78% of its hours, weldments 10% and chassis 6%. Its setups run at 1.03 of standard and its run time at 1.06, so the cell's queue is load, not standards. See Appendix Table 10 for additional detail.

The other work centers

The other work centers hold 40% of queue days between them, and the wait at each is set in one of three ways. At grind and deburr, inspection and pack and hardware (28% of queue days) the wait does not change with load; it is set by the daily dispatch list. At assembly, the manual weld bays and the punch (8% of queue days at the first two; the punch is a first operation and outside that count) the wait is flat until the machines are full and steps up only then (Table 3), which they reach in 11, 3 and 10 weeks of 52; the lasers behave the same way and reach full load only at the year-end build (Flow and On-time Delivery). At the powder line (4% of queue days) the wait is the color schedule. See Appendix Tables 11, 12, 13 and 14 for additional detail.

Queue Days by Work Center and What Sets the Wait, 2025
Shares are of the 36,318 queue days after the first operation; the lasers' and the punch's first-operation queue days are shown beside them and are not in that total.

At the three dispatch-list work centers, whether an operation starts the day it arrives depends on the hour of arrival and not on how busy the work center is. Work arriving before 10:00 starts the same day nine times in ten; work arriving between 10:00 and 14:00, half of all arrivals, starts the same day about three times in ten and otherwise waits for the next morning; the 24% arriving after the shift ends always waits. The brakes show no such break. These next-day starts add 1.3 working days of lead time to a shipped job (1.4 in Q2-Q4). See Appendix Table 15 for additional detail.

The powder line's wait is 1.03 days per operation for the color day, 1.8 to 1.9 days for the three colors run once a week; a third day for black was tested in Options Tested and does not move on-time delivery. See Appendix Table 16 for additional detail.

3. Brake Hours: Setups, Standards and the Peak

The brake queue is set by uneven arrivals and by load above 85%, so what matters at the brakes is hours: how many are lost to setups over standard and how many of those can be recovered, whether the standards the schedule is planned on are right, how far released work exceeds brake capacity at the year-end peak, and where a crewed shift is open. This section measures each from the record. Options Tested takes these measured hours as inputs and tests, on the shop model, what changing them does to on-time delivery, alongside release and dispatch rules. One measured quantity is set aside here: halving the spread of setup time (each setup time moved halfway to the mean, the curve rescaled for the lower variability of setup and run) releases 0.8 brake hours a week and cuts the queue time on the curve from 3.08 to 2.98 days. The mean setup time is a different matter, and is where the hours are.

Brake setups

27,171 setups at all work centers in 2025, 5,354 of them at the brakes. A setup's ratio is its setup machine hours over the setup standard on the job operation; hours over standard is total setup hours over total standard hours. Every work center except the brakes runs its setups at a median of 0.97 to 1.00 of standard and within 44 overrun hours for the year; the brakes ran 6,077 hours against 4,235 standard, 1,842 over, 35 a week, 11% of brake machine time. The median brake setup runs at 1.16 of standard and the 90th percentile at 2.07. Setup ratios do not move with the 2024 year-end build: the median is 1.17 in the first quarter and 1.16 in Q2-Q4. See Appendix Tables 17 and 18 for additional detail.

Where the overrun sits

Setups handed over at a shift boundary (setup transactions by different operators one after the other across the 14:00 or 22:30 shift boundary; two operators at the same time is a two-person setup, not a handover) are 13% of setups and 49% of the overrun hours, median 1.77 against 1.10; most of those hours are long setups that reached the boundary rather than the handover itself (The setup reduction list and the handover). Lots under 25 are 49% of setups and 66% of the overrun, median 1.28 against 1.02 to 1.05 on larger lots: the setup standards under-plan small lots. First setups of a part by the operator run at 1.28 against 1.06. Enclosures and chassis run at 1.33 and 1.29; panels and brackets at 1.06 and 1.07. See Appendix Tables 19, 20, 21 and 22 for additional detail.

Brake Setup Overrun Hours by Condition

The setup standard on lots under 25 pieces should be raised by 24%, so the schedule and the quote stop under-planning them.

Operator tenure and familiarity

Operators with under 12 months run 16% above tenured operators before controls and 5% after familiarity, grouping, machine, lot size, bend count and shift handover. Their 636 setups of 5,354 were made by 4 operators, 400 by one, and 67% were on B4. The tenure effect is mostly what new operators are given, not who they are. See Appendix Table 23 for additional detail.

Tenure Effect on Brake Setup Ratio as Controls Are Added

1,079 first setups of a repeat part were made while another current brake operator had set the part up in the previous 24 months. They ran at 1.56 of standard against 1.28 for setups with prior familiarity in the same lot band and family: 4.4 brake hours a week (3.1 where that operator was on the same shift). This is a matched comparison, not a start-time test, so part of it may be selection. Repeat setups should go to an operator who has set the part up before. See Appendix Table 24 for additional detail.

Grouping

38% of brake setups follow a job on the same tooling set and run at 0.92 of standard against 1.33, saving 5.4 hours a week. B1 and B2 group 26% to 27% of their setups against 42% to 46% on B3 to B5: precision work and the hot list come first there. See Appendix Table 25 for additional detail.

Same-tooling Grouping by Brake

The setup reduction list and the handover

The top 12 part-operations release 7.3 brake hours a week at standard: 21% of the overrun and 2.3% of brake machine time. The overrun is spread across 1,980 part-operations, so no short list captures most of it. See Appendix Tables 26 and 27 for additional detail.

Handed-over setups carry 16.2 hours a week above the ratio of setups finished by the operator who started them, but most of that is length, not handover: a long setup is the one that reaches the end of the shift. Setups started in the last 30 minutes of a shift are handed over 79% of the time and run at a median 1.25 against 1.14 to 1.15 for setups started with more than an hour left. On that comparison the handover itself costs 1.2 brake hours a week. See Appendix Table 28 for additional detail.

Three levers release 12.9 brake hours a week, 4.1% of brake machine time: the top 12 part-operations run at standard (7.3 hours a week), repeat setups assigned to an operator who has set the part up before (4.4), and a shift-handover standard at the brakes, the operator who starts a setup finishing it or leaving a written handover at the machine (1.2). The options report carries these hours as the setup program.

Brake Hours a Week Released by Setup Lever, 2025

Planning standards

The standards the schedule and the quote are built on are wrong in two known directions. L2 and L3 run at 0.81 of the run standard and L1 at 1.00; every other work center runs at 1.01 to 1.06. 553 of 1,931 active parts (29%) carry a stale standard (one dated at the part's first quote) and run 7% to 16% further above standard than refreshed parts at the same work center. See Appendix Tables 29 and 30 for additional detail.

Run Actual Over Standard by Work Center

With the stale standards refreshed, planned hours rise 1.7% at the brakes and 0.9% to 3.0% elsewhere; standard hours per job rise 8% on jobs for those parts (24% of jobs) and 1.9% overall. With the L2 and L3 run standards set to measured, planned laser hours fall 13.6% and standard hours per job fall 2.6%. Both together move standard hours per job by -0.7%. The 553 stale standards should be refreshed and the L2 and L3 run standards set to measured, with the effect on standard hours per job going to estimating (Quoting and Early Warning from Load). See Appendix Tables 31 and 32 for additional detail.

The peak and the open shift

The year-end build is a brake-hours gap. In the six weeks from November 11, 2024 the shop released 355 brake standard hours a week against 284 hours of crewed brake capacity net of downtime (weekday shifts, in standard hours at the brakes' 1.16 actual-over-standard ratio for 2024), a shortfall of 70 hours a week that the queue absorbed; the shop ran 2 queue-triggered Saturday brake shifts and 0 extended hours over the period, supplying 28 standard hours. One Saturday brake shift on B1 and B2 supplies 14 standard hours (16 machine hours), 20% of the weekly gap; the options report tests a planned Saturday shift every week from November through February against the queue-triggered practice. See Appendix Table 33 for additional detail.

Brake Hours Released Against Capacity by Week, Q4 2024 to Q1 2025

The robotic weld cell runs one shift at 83% utilization and is at or above 95% in 20 weeks of 52; its second shift is uncrewed. The options report tests and prices crewing it.

The dispatch list

At grind and deburr, inspection and pack and hardware the lever is the list, not capacity. A midday refresh of the day's dispatch list lets the arrivals between 10:00 and 12:00, 21% of the total, start the same afternoon; what arrives after the shift ends can only start the next morning. The gain is lead time rather than on-time delivery, up to 0.3 of the 1.3 working days a shipped job spends in next-day starts.
