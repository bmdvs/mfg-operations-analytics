1. Work in Process

In 2025, the shop carried an average of 238 WIP jobs against a throughput of about 19 shipped a day, so a job spent about 12 working days on the floor. 2025 WIP was skewed by the large Q1 backlog detailed below. WIP averaged 187 jobs over the past three years and peaks every December: 260 jobs in 2023, 466 in 2024 and 418 in 2025.

Average WIP and Working Days on the Floor, 2023 to 2025

WIP peaked at 466 jobs at the end of 2024 against a Q3 2024 average of 150. In Q4 2024, the shop released 26% more brake standard hours than in Q3. This surge pushed the lasers to 92% of scheduled hours on average over the six weeks from November 18, vs. 69% in Q3. Over those weeks, 96 to 223 jobs were waiting to be cut, vs. an average of 35 jobs in Q3. The work then queued at the brakes, which already run at 90% in a normal quarter, and it took through Q1 and into Q2 2025 to clear this backlog. See Appendix Table 1 for additional detail.

Weekly WIP and Net Inflow, Q3 2024 to Q2 2025

By location, the brakes (queue and run together) hold 28% of WIP in 2025, while the laser cutting stations hold 22% and outside processing 10%. The other eight work centers and jobs complete but not yet shipped hold the remaining 40% between them, none above 9%. In Q2-Q4 the brakes' share falls to 17% and the lasers' rises to 24%, as a result of the Q1 backlog having been worked through.

The 2025 average floor WIP of 238 jobs was 0.96x the WIP the quoted lead times imply at measured throughput; in Q2-Q4 it was 202 WIP jobs against 240 implied (0.84x). The excess for the year is a result of the first-quarter backlog. In Q2-Q4 the floor carried 38 fewer jobs than its quoted lead times allow at the throughput it achieved, so it could have taken on about 19% more work, or quoted shorter lead times, and still shipped the average job inside its quote. See Appendix Table 2 for additional detail.

Average Share of Jobs in WIP by Location

WIP was within 10% of its expected value (measured as throughput x working days) in 8 of the last 12 quarters, meaning the floor was in balance. The four quarters that miss are all year-end quarters: the builds (2024 Q4 and 2025 Q4), when WIP ran ahead of shipments, and the quarters in which a build shipped (2023 Q1 and 2025 Q1), when shipments ran ahead of WIP. 2. Lead Time

Lead time stages

A job's lead time is the number of working days from its release onto the floor to its shipment to the customer. A job moves through the stages outlined in the diagram below. First, the traveler is printed and the job waits in queue at its first operation, either the laser cutting station or the punch, and for its material when it is not on hand. It is then cut, and repeats through move, queue, and setup and run at each later work center on its routing. Where the routing calls for it, the job goes out for outside processing and returns. Holds are counted as their own stage at whatever point on the routing they occur. Once the job is complete it waits to ship. Queue is defined as arrival at a work center to first start. Setup and run is measured from first start to completion, and move is measured from the end of one operation to arrival at the next.

Lead time stages, release to shipment

Actual vs. quoted lead times

In 2025, the median job shipped in 11.2 working days vs. an average quoted lead time of 12.9; in Q2-Q4, it was 9.2 vs. 12.5. The quoted lead time was met on 63% of jobs in 2025 and 77% in Q2-Q4. The shop quotes a standard lead time by routing class, 10 working days for repeat parts, 15 for new parts and 20 with outside processing, and adds a week to new quotes once the floor has been backed up for several weeks; the due date is the later of the quote and the customer's requested date. The quote was met on 59% of repeat-part jobs in 2025 (72% in Q2-Q4), 73% of new-part jobs (88%) and 72% of outside-processing jobs (90%). See Appendix Table 4 for additional detail.

81% of jobs shipped by the due date in 2025 and 91% in Q2-Q4, against the shop's 95% target. The 90th-percentile lead time for all jobs is 23.2 days for 2025 and 17.2 in Q2-Q4. Late jobs have a median lead time of 19.2 days against 9.2 for on-time jobs. See Appendix Table 5 for additional detail.

Actual vs. Quoted Lead Times, by Routing Class

Lead time decomposition

In 2025, the brake queue was 24% of all jobs' lead time and accounted for the largest share of the extra days on late jobs: 5.76 days against 2.50 for on-time jobs. This was due to the first quarter's backlog: the 2024 year-end build released more work than the brakes could absorb and put 664 of the year's 987 late jobs into Q1. In Q2-Q4, with the backlog cleared, late jobs waited less at the brakes than on-time jobs (0.73 days against 1.54).

In Q2-Q4, the extra days on late jobs were due to material wait at the first operation (2.35 days for late jobs vs. 0.65 for on-time jobs), outside processing (2.24 vs. 1.17), the robotic weld queue (1.14 vs. 0.17) and setup and run (1.81 vs. 0.66; the average lot size for late jobs was 72 pieces vs. 47 for on-time jobs). See Appendix Tables 6a and 6b for additional detail.

Lead time by stage, on-time vs. late jobs

Across all work centers, for both on-time and late jobs, the brakes accounted for 53% of queue time in 2025 and 35% in Q2-Q4. Grind and deburr follows at 15% in 2025 and 21% in Q2-Q4, and inspection and pack at 11% and 16%. The brake queue per operation has a median of 1.0 day in 2025 vs. 0.5 in Q2-Q4, and a 90th percentile of 9.5 days in 2025 vs. 4.1 in Q2-Q4. See Appendix Table 7 for additional detail.

3. Late Jobs

Drivers behind late jobs

In 2025, the shop had 987 late jobs that shipped a total of 3,473 days late (average of 3.5 days late for each job), with 323 of these late jobs (33%) in Q2-Q4 that shipped a total of 1,035 days late (average of 3.2 days late for each). See Appendix Tables 8a and 8b for lost days by customer.

We analyzed the ERP and shop-floor records to arrive at an attribution of lost days (i.e., how many days late the jobs shipped) by driver. For each late job, we considered how many days it ran longer than an on-time job of the same routing class at each stage, as well as material holds and late purchase order receipts. We then assigned each job to a driver.

In 2025, jobs waiting at work centers (i.e., queue constraints) accounted for 42% of the 3,473 lost days, while jobs that were released late accounted for 21%, and jobs that waited on material and outside processing accounted for 7% and 3%, respectively. In Q2-Q4, jobs that were released late accounted for 48% of the 1,035 lost days, with queue constraint (16%), material (12%) and outside processing (3%) the other main drivers.

Jobs were released late when they went to the floor with fewer working days to the due date than the job's quoted lead time; this was the largest driver on 162 of the 323 (50%) late jobs in Q2-Q4. See Appendix Tables 9, 10a and 10b for additional detail.

24% of lost days in 2025 and 18% in Q2-Q4 have no attribution. Of the 823 non-attributed lost days in 2025, 27% was from work center queue time above on-time median but below the attribution threshold, 30% from setup and run time above normal with no overrun or rework recorded, and 16% from movement between stages. The remainder have no single discernible driver. See Appendix Tables 11 and 12 for additional detail.

The shop's own records

Late-reason codes are inputted retroactively on late jobs by the customer service team during the weekly delivery review. These codes are pulled from a dropdown list with six choices: capacity, material, outside processing, customer change, quality and other. As seen below, a significant portion of late jobs had no late-reason code entered (36% in 2025 and 37% in Q2-Q4). See Appendix Table 13 for additional detail.

Late-reason codes as entered on late jobs

To validate the accuracy of these codes, we compared them against the lost days attribution drivers shown above. We first mapped the late-reason codes to the corresponding attribution drivers: queue constraint and setup overrun count as capacity; material, outside processing and quality as their own codes; a hold and days not attributed as other. The notable exception is that the "released late" attribution driver is not a listed option for late-reason codes; those jobs are mostly coded as capacity or other (112 of the 149 with a code), and count as other in the comparison. We recommend adding this late-reason code into the system, given it accounts for a significant portion of lost days.

As shown below, the shop's entered reason-codes do not adequately capture the true drivers of lost days. The entered code agrees with the largest attributed cause on just 43.9% of coded late jobs against the 35.9% expected had the codes been assigned at random (34.7% against 25.1% in Q2-Q4). Queue constraint is the largest attributed driver on just 54% of the jobs coded capacity, while being misattributed across the rest (making up 50% of the jobs with no code and 19-76% of the other drivers). Material is the largest cause on just 21 of the 78 (27%) jobs coded material, outside processing on none of the 37 coded outside processing (0%) and quality on none of the 27 (0%) coded quality. Of the 241 jobs where released late is the largest attributed driver, 72 are coded capacity, 92 carry no code and 40 are coded other. See Appendix Tables 14 and 15 for additional detail.

To increase the accuracy and usefulness of its late-reason codes, the shop should consider process changes to (i) include additional options (specifically late releases) in the late-reason code dropdown menu, and (ii) enable real-time shop-floor data capture on the reasons for late jobs once they're known.

4. Quoting and Early Warning

Quoting from the brake backlog at release, at or above 2.5 days, would have raised on-time delivery for 2025 from 80.8% to 85.4% and removed 240 late jobs, nearly all among jobs released into the year-end build, while leaving ordinary quarters unchanged. At the win rate the shop loses on longer quotes, it would also have cost about 320 orders, $1.46M of revenue. The same backlog flags the jobs most likely to ship late at release, and the on-time start rate warns of a decline a week ahead.

A quote from the brake backlog

The shop's adder raised quotes from December 16, 4 weeks after the quote table's quotes first rose in the week of November 18. The table quotes each non-rush job the 80th-percentile lead time of earlier jobs of its routing class released at the same brake backlog, never below the class standard; the recommended rule quotes from it at or above 2.5 days of backlog and quotes the class standard below. The table as first fitted quoted 13.5 days in the three weeks from January 13 against backlogs of 7 to 8 days because no earlier job had shipped from a backlog above 5 days; a band with fewer than 30 earlier jobs now takes the quote of the band below it, which raises those quotes to 19 to 20 days. See Appendix Tables 16 and 17 for additional detail.

On-time Delivery by Release Quarter, Current Practice Against the Two Quote Rules

What a longer quote costs

Every day added to a quote costs orders, and the recommended rule spends them only in a build. Non-rush quotes 5 days above the class standard won 36.9% against 46.2% at the standard, 1.85 points a day; the fall is measured only at 5 days, on the 1,075 quotes that carried the shop's adder, and is charged in proportion to the days added. At that rate the recommended rule forgoes about 320 orders to remove 240 late jobs in 2025 and none in an ordinary year, and the shop's own adder forgoes about 100. Quote turnaround costs more than the rule: quotes sent after more than 3 working days win 13.3 points less after adjusting for RFQ complexity, about 427 orders and $2.2M a year. On rush RFQs the win rate falls from 54.3% at a quoted lead time of 5 days or fewer to 23.9% at 12 or more. 40% of lost quotes carry no reason; every lost quote should carry one. See Appendix Tables 20, 21, 22, 23, 24 and 25 for additional detail. Win rates by customer, key account and estimator are in Appendix Table 26.

Late Jobs Removed and Orders Forgone by Quote Rule, 2025

Flags at release

Two flags at release mark the jobs most likely to ship late: brake backlog in a build, a short due date in an ordinary quarter. Jobs released above 3 days of brake backlog are 22% of jobs released in 2025 and 50% of late jobs, a late rate of 31% against 9%; no job released in Q2-Q4 crossed it. Jobs released late are 19% of jobs released in Q2-Q4 and 51% of late jobs, a late rate of 23.5% against 5.3%. Each late release should be a decision: flag the job as a rush, with a named approver, and count these releases weekly. See Appendix Tables 27, 28 and 29 for additional detail.

Weekly indicators

The on-time start rate leads on-time delivery by one week in ordinary weeks (correlation 0.70, against chance levels of 0.26 and 0.27). It moved 8, 2 and 8 weeks before the three declines in on-time delivery, which cannot be told apart from chance (1.4 and 1.3 expected). Jobs waiting at the lasers moved 7 weeks before the December 2024 decline and 8 weeks before the October 2025 decline, 2 of 3 and no better than chance. Outside-processing receipts on time clear both chance levels only at a 12-week lead (correlation 0.24); no other measure leads in ordinary weeks.
