1. Flow and WIP

Every job follows the same general flow through the shop between its release onto the floor and its shipment to the customer. At any moment every job in WIP sits somewhere in this flow, and its lead time, the working days from release to shipment, is the time it takes to pass through it.

Job Flow, Release to Shipment

In 2025, the shop carried an average of 238 WIP jobs against a throughput of about 19 shipped a day, so a job spent about 12 working days on the floor. 2025 WIP was skewed by the large Q1 backlog detailed below. WIP averaged 187 jobs over the past three years and peaks every December: 260 jobs in 2023, 466 in 2024 and 418 in 2025.

Average WIP and Working Days on the Floor, 2023 to 2025

WIP peaked at 466 jobs at the end of 2024 against a Q3 2024 average of 150. In Q4 2024, the shop released 26% more brake standard hours than in Q3. This surge pushed the lasers to 92% of scheduled hours on average over the six weeks from November 18, vs. 69% in Q3. Over those weeks, 96 to 223 jobs were waiting to be cut, vs. an average of 35 jobs in Q3. The work then queued at the brakes, which already run at 90% in a normal quarter, and it took through Q1 and into Q2 2025 to clear this backlog. See Appendix Table 1 for additional detail.

Weekly WIP and Net Inflow, Q3 2024 to Q2 2025

By location, the brakes (queue and run together) hold 28% of WIP in 2025, while the laser cutting stations hold 22% and outside processing 10%. The other eight work centers and jobs complete but not yet shipped hold the remaining 40% between them, none above 9%. In Q2-Q4 the brakes' share falls to 17% and the lasers' rises to 24%, as a result of the Q1 backlog having been worked through.

Average Share of Jobs in WIP by Location

The 2025 average floor WIP of 238 jobs was 0.96x the WIP the quoted lead times imply at measured throughput; in Q2-Q4 it was 202 WIP jobs against 240 implied (0.84x). The excess for the year is a result of the first-quarter backlog. In Q2-Q4 the floor carried 38 fewer jobs than its quoted lead times allow at the throughput it achieved, so it could have taken on about 19% more work, or quoted shorter lead times, and still shipped the average job inside its quote. See Appendix Table 2 for additional detail.

2. Lead Time

Lead time decomposition

In 2025, the average lead time of all jobs was 12.8 days, and in Q2-Q4 it was 10.3 days. 84% of lead time in 2025 was spent waiting (movement between and queueing at work centers), against 6% spent working at machines (both setup and run), and 10% with outside vendors. The time spent waiting was concentrated: in 2025, the brake queue alone was 3.1 days of the average job's lead time (24% of total), and the brakes held 53% of all queue time. The brake queue time in 2025 was highly skewed: the median queue time was 1.0 day, with a 90th percentile of 9.5 days. We've presented Q2-Q4 data alongside 2025, as this largely removes the impact of the Q4 build and presents a more normalized view. See Appendix Table 3 for additional detail.

Lead Time Decomposition by Stage, 2025 and Q2-Q4

Actual vs. quoted lead times

In 2025, the median job shipped in 11.2 working days vs. an average quoted lead time of 12.9; in Q2-Q4, it was 9.2 vs. 12.5. The quoted lead time was met on 63% of jobs in 2025 and 77% in Q2-Q4. The quote was met on 59% of repeat-part jobs in 2025 (72% in Q2-Q4), 73% of new-part jobs (88%) and 72% of outside-processing jobs (90%). See Appendix Table 4 for additional detail.

The shop quotes lead times by starting with a routing class standard (10 working days for repeat parts, 15 for new parts and 20 with outside processing) which is then adjusted slightly based on current shop capacity and WIP levels. Section 4 analyzes the quote against the brake backlog.

3. Late Jobs

A job's due date is set as the later of the quote and the customer's requested date. Requested dates were 74% of job due dates in 2025, with a median of 15 working days. On-time delivery was 81% in 2025, and in Q2-Q4 once the backlog was worked through, on-time delivery was 91%.

There were 987 late jobs in 2025, with a median days late of 3 and a 90th percentile of 7. That dropped to 323 late jobs in Q2-Q4, with a median days late of 2 and a 90th percentile of 7. In 2025, 40% of jobs shipped 6 days or more before the due date. See Appendix Tables 5 and 6 for additional detail.

Working Days Shipped After the Due Date, 2025 and Q2-Q4

Where late jobs spend their time

In 2025, the brake queue was 24% of all jobs' lead time and accounted for the largest share of the extra days on late jobs: 5.76 days against 2.50 for on-time jobs. This was due to the first quarter's backlog: the 2024 year-end build released more work than the brakes could absorb and put 664 of the year's 987 late jobs into Q1. In Q2-Q4, with the backlog cleared, late jobs waited less at the brakes than on-time jobs (0.73 days against 1.54).

In Q2-Q4, the extra days on late jobs were due to material wait at the first operation (2.35 days for late jobs vs. 0.65 for on-time jobs), outside processing (2.24 vs. 1.17), the robotic weld queue (1.14 vs. 0.17) and setup and run (1.81 vs. 0.66; the average lot size for late jobs was 72 pieces vs. 47 for on-time jobs). See Appendix Tables 7a and 7b for additional detail.

Drivers behind late jobs

We analyzed the ERP and shop-floor records to arrive at an attribution of lost days (i.e., how many days late the jobs shipped) by driver. For each late job, we considered how many days it ran longer than an on-time job of the same routing class at each stage, as well as material holds and late purchase order receipts. We then assigned each job to a driver.

In 2025, jobs waiting at work centers (i.e., queue constraints) accounted for 42% of the 3,473 lost days, while jobs that were released late accounted for 21%, and jobs that waited on material and outside processing accounted for 7% and 3%, respectively. In Q2-Q4, jobs that were released late accounted for 48% of the 1,035 lost days, with queue constraint (16%), material (12%) and outside processing (3%) the other main drivers. Jobs were released late when they went to the floor with fewer working days to the due date than the job's quoted lead time; this was the largest driver on 162 of the 323 (50%) late jobs in Q2-Q4. See Appendix Tables 8, 9a and 9b for additional detail.

24% of lost days in 2025 and 18% in Q2-Q4 have no attribution. Of the 823 non-attributed lost days in 2025, 27% was from work center queue time above on-time median but below the attribution threshold, 30% from setup and run time above normal with no overrun or rework recorded, and 16% from movement between stages. The remainder have no single discernible driver. See Appendix Tables 10 and 11 for additional detail.

The shop's own records

Late-reason codes are inputted retroactively on late jobs by the customer service team during the weekly delivery review. These codes are pulled from a dropdown list with six choices: capacity, material, outside processing, customer change, quality and other. As seen below, a significant portion of late jobs had no late-reason code entered (36% in 2025 and 37% in Q2-Q4). See Appendix Table 12 for additional detail.

Late-reason codes as entered on late jobs

To validate the accuracy of these codes, we compared them against the lost days attribution drivers shown above. We first mapped the late-reason codes to the corresponding attribution drivers: queue constraint and setup overrun count as capacity; material, outside processing and quality as their own codes; a hold and days not attributed as other. The notable exception is that the "released late" attribution driver is not a listed option for late-reason codes; those jobs are mostly coded as capacity or other (112 of the 149 with a code), and count as other in the comparison. We recommend adding this late-reason code into the system, given it accounts for a significant portion of lost days.

As shown below, the shop's entered reason-codes do not adequately capture the true drivers of lost days. The entered code agrees with the largest attributed cause on just 43.9% of coded late jobs against the 35.9% expected had the codes been assigned at random (34.7% against 25.1% in Q2-Q4). Queue constraint is the largest attributed driver on just 54% of the jobs coded capacity, while being misattributed across the rest (making up 50% of the jobs with no code and 19-76% of the other drivers). Material is the largest cause on just 21 of the 78 (27%) jobs coded material, outside processing on none of the 37 coded outside processing (0%) and quality on none of the 27 (0%) coded quality. Of the 241 jobs where released late is the largest attributed driver, 72 are coded capacity, 92 carry no code and 40 are coded other. See Appendix Tables 13 and 14 for additional detail.

What the shop coded against what the data shows

To increase the accuracy and usefulness of its late-reason codes, the shop should consider process changes to (i) include additional options (specifically late releases) in the late-reason code dropdown menu, and (ii) enable real-time shop-floor data capture on the reasons for late jobs once they're known.

4. Quoting

The shop's quote misses the lead time a job actually takes by 4.8 working days on average, and in opposite directions: in ordinary months it is 2.5 days too long, with the median repeat part shipping in 8 days against a 10-day standard, and in the four months of the 2024 year-end build it was 5.8 days too short. A quote set from what jobs of the same routing class actually take cuts the miss in ordinary months from 4.4 to 3.3 days. Adding the brake backlog at release lets the quote lengthen when the brakes load up. The record could teach that only once it held a build, so out of sample it would not have helped in 2024; fitted on 2023 to 2025, it misses by 5.6 days in the build against 7.3 for the current quote. See Appendix Table 15 for additional detail.

How accurate the quote is

The quote barely moves while lead time swings with the brake backlog. The mean quote stays between 12.0 and 15.0 days while the mean lead time runs from 7.9 to 22.8; the quote is too long in 30 of the 35 months and too short from November 2024 to February 2025. See Appendix Table 16 for additional detail.

Quoted and Actual Lead Time by Release Month, Non-rush Jobs, 2023 to 2025

When the quote misses

Brake backlog at release explains 23% of the error. The quote runs 3.1 days long below 1 day of backlog and 5.6 to 6.2 days short above 3, across all three years, not only in the build. Outside processing quotes run 4.2 days long: the median job takes 14 days against the 20-day standard. Job size and lot size explain under 1%. The backlog at release sees a build late: jobs released in November 2024 ran 3.5 days over their quotes at 1.4 days of backlog. See Appendix Table 17 for additional detail.

Quote Error by Condition at Release, Non-rush Jobs, 2023 to 2025

A more accurate quote

The recommended quote is the median lead time of jobs of the same routing class at the same brake backlog at release, refitted monthly from the jobs shipped; the table below gives it as fitted on 2023 to 2025. Fitted that way, it misses by 3.2 days in ordinary months and 5.6 in the build, against 4.4 and 7.3 for today's quote, and quotes shorter than today on 84% of jobs, by 3.9 days on average, and longer on 14%, by 3.6. Applied month by month as the shop would have had it at the time, it does as well in ordinary months (3.4 days) but not in the 2024 build (8.9), because no earlier job had shipped from a backlog above 3 days. A median quote is met by about half of jobs; at the 80th percentile of the same cell the commitment is met on 82% of jobs, against 72% for today's quote. See Appendix Tables 18, 19 and 20 for additional detail.

5. Early Warning

Two flags at release mark the jobs most likely to ship late, and one weekly measure warns of a decline in on-time delivery a week ahead.

Flags at release

Brake backlog flags jobs in a build, and a short due date flags them in an ordinary quarter. Jobs released above 3 days of brake backlog are 22% of jobs released in 2025 and 50% of late jobs, a late rate of 31% against 9%; no job released in Q2-Q4 crossed it. Jobs released late are 19% of jobs released in Q2-Q4 and 51% of late jobs, a late rate of 23.5% against 5.3%. Each late release should be a decision: flag the job as a rush, with a named approver, and count these releases weekly. See Appendix Tables 21, 22 and 23 for additional detail.

Late Rate by Brake Backlog at Release

Weekly indicators

The on-time start rate leads on-time delivery by one week in ordinary weeks (correlation 0.70, against chance levels of 0.26 and 0.27). It moved 8, 2 and 8 weeks before the three declines in on-time delivery, which cannot be told apart from chance (1.4 and 1.3 expected). Jobs waiting at the lasers moved 7 weeks before the December 2024 decline and 8 weeks before the October 2025 decline, 2 of 3 and no better than chance. Outside-processing receipts on time clear both chance levels only at a 12-week lead (correlation 0.24); no other measure leads in ordinary weeks. See Appendix Tables 24, 25, 26, 27 and 28 for additional detail.

Flag at release every job above 3 days of brake backlog and every job released late, and review the on-time start rate weekly.
